# Epic: research notes

Started 2026-09-09. Research behind the roadmap's epic skill: what an epic is for, what it carries, and how its success is stated. The four sources in the reading plan and the wayfinder skill were read 2026-09-18, one agent per source; what each says, what an epic carries and never carries, how its success is stated, where they disagree, and the open decisions are written below.

## References

Local copies are markdown files in `~/Downloads/books/`, one file per book. Primary first. Each line says what the book has an opinion on here.

- **User Story Mapping**, Jeff Patton. An epic is a slice of the map with an outcome, released by outcome rather than by feature set.
- **Evidence Guided**, Itamar Gilad. Goals to ideas to steps: an epic carries a target outcome, its leading indicators, and a confidence level in the evidence behind it.
- **Empowered**, Marty Cagan and Chris Jones. A team objective is a problem handed to a team with key results, not a roadmap of features with dates handed to it. Local copy is the 2021 Wiley edition, the only one.
- **Measure What Matters**, John Doerr. The objective and key results that say what the epic is meant to move.
- **Lean Analytics**, Alistair Croll and Benjamin Yoskovitz. The one metric an epic exists to move at this stage.
- **Continuous Discovery Habits**, Teresa Torres. The opportunity above a set of stories, with the desired outcome above that.

## What each source says

Read 2026-09-18, one agent per source, whole book unless the reading plan says a chapter. Quotes are from the local copies; chapter names are the copies' own. None of the four books uses "epic" as its own unit; each was read for its equivalent, named first.

### User Story Mapping

His equivalent is the opportunity, or the release slice with its target outcome. The word epic appears once and he renames it.

- "An epic is a story that we expect is large, and know needs to be broken down." It is "the right size from a business, customer, or user perspective, just not from a development perspective." "Keep the epic around because you'll need to speak to people about it" (Ch 11, "Rock Breaking"). "In story lingo, you could call them epics, but I prefer to call them opportunities. No matter what you call them, they're still stories, and the goal of the first conversations we have about them is to decide whether to move forward with them, or trash them" (Ch 13, "Start with Opportunities").
- Three sizes, one word: right-sized for a user "fulfills a need," for a developer "takes just a few days to build and test," for the business "helps a business achieve a business outcome." "The right size is the size that's relevant to the conversation you're having" (Ch 11). Stakeholders read the business size: "They likely aren't interested in whether you meet your plan for the bunch of single, small parts you built. What they're interested in is progress on the minimum viable solution" (Ch 11).
- Its physical form is a sticky at the left edge of a slice: "a few words describing their intention for each release slice, their target outcomes" (Ch 2); or "a few words about what they want to learn in this release" (Ch 3). The release slice is "focused on reaching specific outcomes for a target group of users" (Ch 11).
- The opportunity discussion covers five things: who, "often different groups"; what problems, "how they solve their problems today"; what we're imagining; why, "how we imagine it'll benefit our organization"; size, "days, weeks, or months" (Ch 13). "I'm not saying we need to calculate return on investment, because anyone who can do that at this stage is likely full of something other than just stories" (Ch 13).
- On the card at this level: metrics named before release, "if you really care about results, identify specific metrics you'll track after the software is released" (Ch 8); the framing artifacts, "named business problems, specific business metrics affected, short lists of specific customers and users, metrics that will allow us to measure whether people use and like this new feature, big risks and assumptions" (Ch 14, table); a budget, "estimate well enough to set a budget for delivering the solution" (Ch 14).
- Children are bullets under a distilled title: "The distillations become your new, bigger stories. The other cards become bullet points in its description." "'UI improvements' may be too vague. 'Improve entering and editing comments' would be better" (Ch 17). Whitespace is content: "Having the activity with no tasks in it lets you show that you aren't going to hit that goal this morning" (Ch 5).
- Never: a feature list, "it's a list of real-world benefits" (Ch 2); the requirements document, "you're at the point where lots of people celebrate because they've 'finished the requirements.' Don't be those people" (Ch 14); pre-split children, "your backlog will fill with lots of small stories flying every which way, and you'll die" (Ch 17); only stories, "if the only thing you create while making sense of a big, ambiguous opportunity is smaller stories, then you're probably doing it wrong" (Ch 14). The one-to-five-page narratives were "largely ignored" (Ch 16).
- Success: "We measure what people actually do differently to reach their goals as a consequence of what you've built" (Read This First). A target outcome names people, a moment, and a behavior: "behind outcomes are specific behavior changes for specific people engaged in specific activities" (Ch 2). "Where you see the words 'business value,' you're supposed to fill that in with something specific" (Ch 14). The minimum viable solution "is the smallest solution release that successfully achieves its desired outcomes," and in the next breath, "We're just guessing" (Ch 2). Done is a grade per activity, "if we had to ship right now, what grade would we give ourselves?" (Ch 18). "Outcomes are never insured" (Ch 18); "around 20 percent max" of what is built succeeds (Ch 15).
- Assumptions: "What are our biggest, riskiest assumptions? Where is the uncertainty?" (Ch 2). "A bulleted list of the things we believe are true, but if we find they aren't, then we'll need to rethink everything" (Ch 15); JSTOR's is two short lists, one about users and one about the solution. Two kinds: "concerns about what users will like and adopt, and real technical feasibility concerns" (Ch 17). "If you catch yourself saying, 'There's not much risk or uncertainty in this project,' those are famous last words" (Ch 15).
- The children borrow their why from above: "the users' bigger goal is often in the card above the one you're viewing" (Ch 7). Most children are discarded: "If you're not cutting away more ideas than you keep, you're probably not doing discovery work right" (Ch 14). Parentless work is fine: "Don't Sweat the Small Stuff" (Ch 17).
- His concession for our shop: "I know your company probably doesn't use opportunities. You've got a roadmap filled with stuff you're supposed to build. Making a no-go decision on any of that stuff may not be your call." Fallback: "make sure you leave the conversation with shared understanding about the problems you're solving, for whom, and how your organization benefits." When the decision maker is absent: "have the conversation anyway, and make assumptions about who, what, and why. Then share those assumptions with decision makers. I promise you that they'll correct you if you've got it wrong" (Ch 13). "We're often given a feature or enhancement idea and then need to work backward to understand the problem. Start with what you have" (Ch 13).
- Against the tool: "Shared documents aren't shared understanding" (Read This First). "It's clear to you because your really smart brain is filling in all the details that aren't written down" (Ch 9). "The go/no-go decision to proceed with an opportunity falls to the product owner" (Ch 13). "Start by guessing. In the bad old days, you'd have guessed and pretended you weren't. So just stop pretending" (Ch 15). "Don't use the term epic as a stick to reprimand someone else" (Ch 11).

### Evidence Guided

His equivalent is the idea, read with the goal above it. The idea carries hypothesis, confidence, and evidence; the outcome and the why live on the goal.

- "Ideas are hypothetical ways to achieve the goals. The key word here is hypothetical. As most ideas don't work, we have to test multiple ideas and invest only in those that yield supporting evidence" (Ch 1, "The GIST Model"). Jira epics sit lower in his vocabulary: a test step can be written "as one or more user stories or epics that describe the experience of the participant" (Ch 5).
- The idea record lives in an idea bank, "not backlogs, meaning we don't expect to implement all or even most of the ideas they hold." Entering one is "a weak form of commitment." A dead idea stays: "move it to the Parked state. Perhaps in the future someone will come up with a better version of the idea and benefit from what you've learned" (Ch 3, Ch 4). "Technical ideas that don't directly impact any of the team's metrics, re-factor module X, should not be placed in the idea bank" (Ch 3).
- Carries: the idea in a line and its state with reasons; the target metric the bank is ranked against; ICE, three numbers 0 to 10 or "Low/Medium/High"; confidence with its evidence named, in his own communicated form, "Confidence in these impact and ease assessment is medium-low (~2 out of 10). Our strongest evidence is customer support requests, and sporadic sales requests" (Ch 3); "links to past steps, results, and insights" (Ch 4). Rescored "at the end of each successful step" (Ch 4).
- The why sits on the goal as OKR context, "a short explanation as to why you think this goal is important, what relevant evidence you've found" (Ch 2); the worked OKR is "short on key results but long on context" with "a link to an outside document" (Ch 6). No idea one-pager exists in the book; the nearest is the step doc and the team that replaced "detailed tickets" with "high-level 1-pagers and low-fidelity mockups" (Ch 6).
- Never: dates, "picking an idea does not mean committing to building and launching it," commitments "the exception rather than the rule" (Ch 3, Ch 4); the idea inside the goal, "the team will be motivated to focus on output, delivering on the plan" (Ch 2); detail, "the more detailed you make the requirements and user stories, the less likely you are to get what you expect" (Ch 5); the full plan, "we'll only need the next 2 to 4 steps per idea" (Ch 5); theme alignment as justification, "thematic support yields very little extra confidence" (Ch 3).
- Success: "Goals define the outcomes we wish to achieve, measurable benefits for customers and for the business"; output goals are "very common in the industry and very harmful" (Ch 1). Key results carry baseline and target, "from 8.5% to 10%" (Ch 2). Metric trees break trailing indicators into ones "you can influence more readily" (Ch 2). TBD is allowed: "KR: TBD. We're planning to conduct research to determine which metrics we should improve," with a date to fill it (Ch 2). "No more than four key results across one or two objectives. Fewer is better" (Ch 2). Missing a target is not punished, "that will just demotivate setting honest goals" (Ch 2).
- Confidence, the scale as used in his worked examples: self-conviction 0.01; thematic support 0.05; others' opinion 0.1; estimates and plans 0.3; anecdotal evidence 0.5; market data 1.0; user evidence 2.0; test results 3.0; launch data the rest. Opinions alone total "0.16 out of 10, or near-zero"; opinion plus anecdote "0.61, still low"; one passed test lifts it past 5 (Ch 3, Ch 4). "In no case should you consider the Confidence Meter a checklist" (Ch 3). Build at "at least medium-high confidence levels (3.0+) before you switch into delivery mode" (Ch 4); cheap and low-risk ideas can stop "even at expert opinion" (Ch 3). Written in the open as a word: "2-Factor Authentication (High confidence) ETA: 15 March. P-auth support (Medium confidence). Security Audits (Low confidence)"; only the high one has a date (Ch 6).
- Assumptions: the kinds are Cagan's four, value, usability, feasibility, viability; no ethical kind (Ch 4). No count; "for complex ideas I recommend Bland's Assumption Mapping" (Ch 4). Listed per step as "What do we need to test?", one or two each, not as a register on the idea (Ch 5). Riskiest first (Ch 1).
- Below the idea are steps, "short projects or activities that develop an idea somewhat (often with no coding) and test it," minutes to weeks; the last step is delivery (Ch 1, Ch 4). Tasks link to steps: "keep managing tasks in Jira, but start referencing or linking back to the steps" (Ch 5). "There should be no hidden projects" (Ch 5). Once developers design the step, "the need for fine-grained user stories drops dramatically" (Ch 5).
- Where it breaks: "GIST may not work in a very traditional organization where decisions strictly flow top-down, and where the function of the product org is merely 'delivery'" (Ch 1). In feature teams, "the value and business viability are the responsibility of the stakeholder or executive that requested the feature" (Ch 8). His script for a handed-down output goal: "interview them and get to the real goal. What are we trying to achieve? What metrics would improve if we're successful? Be careful not to seem as if you're challenging their authority" (Ch 2). "Anyone can assess ideas on paper using ICE, assumption mapping, and stakeholder reviews" (Ch 9). Gaming: "'The opinion of the CMO should be considered market data'" (Ch 9). "The one number you should be most suspicious of is the ICE score"; "consider ICE scores as mere hints" (Ch 3).
- Against the tool: "that's exactly the goal of ICE, to facilitate a structured discussion" (Ch 3). A pitch deck "forces you to think deeper and broader. However, you're still in one-person-opinion land, hence near-zero confidence" (Ch 3), so nothing a tool writes can raise confidence above 0.1. "If you find that you're spending hours each week on ICE-scoring ideas, or taking six steps to validate a one-week feature, you're probably doing it wrong" (Ch 9). "Data is not the same as evidence" (Ch 4).

### Continuous Discovery Habits

Her equivalent is the target opportunity: one leaf need chosen by compare and contrast, carrying the one solution that survived assumption testing. The outcome sits above it and is shared by its siblings.

- "Customer needs, pain points, and desires collectively as 'opportunities'" (Ch 2). Worded in the customer's words: "I can't find anything to watch." "Opportunities don't need to be exact quotes, but you should frame them using your customer's words" (Ch 5). The test: "Is there more than one way to address this opportunity? Otherwise, it's simply a solution in disguise" (Ch 6). Three gates: framed as a need not a solution; heard in more than one interview; "if we address this opportunity, will it drive our desired outcome?" (Ch 6).
- Size is a leaf: "You always want to choose a leaf-node opportunity because our goal is to deliver iterative value over time." A leaf "simply means that you haven't (yet) identified any sub-opportunities" (Ch 7). "'The interface is hard to use' is way too broad" (Ch 6). "No opportunity should have two parents. If it should ladder up to more than one parent, it's framed too broadly" (Ch 6). Siblings are "distinct in that you can address one without addressing another" (Ch 6).
- Life: "your next few days or weeks. You are not committing to addressing that opportunity. You are simply committing to exploring it further"; "if you got it wrong, we'll simply revisit the decision" (Ch 7). One team "invested only a week into an opportunity that wasn't going to be fruitful" (Ch 12).
- Carries: the outcome above, "the root of the tree is your desired outcome" (Ch 2); parent and siblings, and children solved in succession; the evidence, interview snapshots as "a reference or index to the customer knowledge bank" (Ch 5); the solution set, three from fifteen to twenty, "each idea should have a strong advocate" (Ch 8); a story map per solution of use, not build (Ch 9); the assumption list from it, "from a simple 5-step story map, we generated 20 assumptions" (Ch 9); the two or three leap-of-faith ones; success criteria per test set before it runs; the why chain, "This solution will address the target opportunity because... Addressing the target opportunity will drive the desired outcome because..." (Ch 9); instrumentation for the tests and then for the outcome (Ch 11).
- Never: a solution posing as a need, "'I wish I could fast-forward through commercials' is really a solution request" (Ch 6); a feature request unconverted, "If you had that feature, what would that do for you?" (Ch 5); company wording, "No customer would ever say, 'I wish I had more streaming-entertainment subscriptions'" (Ch 6); feelings, "We can't fix feelings" (Ch 6); a roadmap as the conclusion, "we are inviting our stakeholders to an opinion battle, a battle we have no chance of winning" (Ch 13); "a fixed roadmap communicates false certainty" (Ch 3); every heard opportunity (Ch 6); stakeholder ideas in "your development backlog" rather than the idea backlog (Ch 13).
- Success: "A business outcome measures how well the business is progressing. A product outcome measures how well the product is moving the business forward. A traction metric measures usage of a specific feature." Teams take product outcomes, "within the product trio's span of control" (Ch 3). Leading over lagging: "increase the number of dogs who like the food" (Ch 3). A number, a direction, a behavior: "make sure your outcome represents a number even if you aren't sure yet how to measure it"; "An outcome is a change in human behavior that drives business results" (Ch 3). "If one person did many actions, does that create as much value as many people doing one action?" (Ch 11). One at a time with a health metric, "increase acquisition without negatively impacting satisfaction" (Ch 3). A learning goal before a performance goal (Ch 3). Held "for a few quarters"; switching incurs "a learning tax" (Ch 3). Traction metrics "only when you are optimizing a solution" (Ch 3). The link to the business outcome "is a theory that needs to be tested" (Ch 11).
- Assumptions, the five kinds: desirability, "Does anyone want it?"; viability, "Should we build it? enough value for the business to be worth the effort to create and maintain"; feasibility, "Can we build it? will our legal or security team allow for it? Does it comply with regulations?"; usability, "Can customers find what they need? Is it accessible?"; ethical, "Is there any potential harm?" (Ch 9). "How we categorize them doesn't really matter. What matters is that we enumerate them, question them, and, when needed, test them" (Ch 9, note). Twenty to thirty per idea; "most will be true enough" (Ch 9). Phrased as what must be true: "Customers will remember their passwords" (Ch 9). Mapped importance against evidence in "no more than ten minutes per idea"; test "the two or three in the upper-right corner" (Ch 9). Criteria as counts: "At least 3 out of 10 people choose sports"; percentages are "ambiguous" (Ch 10). "Our goal as a product team is not to seek truth but to mitigate risk" (Ch 10). A shared assumption that fails kills the whole set (Ch 10).
- Below: the story map of use is the bridge to requirements, "remember to story map your ideas" (Ch 14); "You aren't story mapping what it will take to implement an idea" (Ch 9). Production tests are where delivery begins; "You are never done with discovery" (Ch 11). Every backlog item should trace to an opportunity; when mapped, "odds are we'll have only one or two solutions for each opportunity" (Ch 8). Small solutions "chip away at the larger opportunity" (Ch 6).
- The tree: visual, never a document; weekly; "structure gets done, undone, and redone" (Ch 6); "If you do your first tree in 30 minutes and think you are done, you are probably not thinking hard enough. They churn for hours trying to create the perfect tree. We don't want to do that, either" (Ch 6). The write-once failure: "it can start to feel like truth" (Ch 4). Its shape diagnoses: shallow means interview more, sprawling means narrow (Ch 2). For a CEO, a paragraph: the outcome, three opportunities, why this one, the top solution, one or two test results (Ch 13).
- Where it breaks: "Many product teams aren't allowed to do discovery. They still work in a feature-team or delivery-team model, where business stakeholders tell them what to build" (Ch 14); delivering outputs is "by far, the most common scenario" (Ch 3). Work backward: "If our customers had this solution, what would it do for them? Try to uncover the implied opportunity. Even if it's a wild guess." "If we shipped this feature, what value would it create for our business? Refine your answer until you get to a clear metric. By asking those two questions, you've also built your first opportunity solution tree" (Ch 14). "When a stakeholder brings a solution to you, story map and identify assumptions with them. Document that conversation. Start doing post-release impact reviews" (Ch 14). Questions for leadership: "Who is the target customer? What business outcome? Why do we think this initiative will drive that outcome? (Be careful with Why? questions.)" (Ch 3). "Choose your battles" (Ch 13).
- Against the tool: "Language is vague. Drawing is more specific. It forces you to be concrete" (Ch 4). "Once we mathematize this process, we'll stop thinking and go strictly by the numbers"; "this is a messy, subjective decision, and you want to keep it that way" (Ch 7). "Declarations of high confidence mainly tell you that an individual has constructed a coherent story in his mind" (Ch 5). "Have we heard this in interviews? Are we just wishing a customer would say this?" (Ch 6). "If one person is the 'voice of the customer,' that role will trump every other role" (Ch 5). "The last thing I wanted was to create a dependency on me" (Ch 2).

### Empowered, Part VII

His unit is the team objective, a problem to solve with key results, set for a quarter. It sits at or above where a Jira epic sits; one objective may run several solutions through discovery. The reading plan's line was checked: the nearest real wording is "whether you give your product teams features to build, or problems to solve" (Ch 50) and "giving them a problem to solve rather than a feature to build" (Ch 53); his contrast object is "a roadmap of features and projects with expected release dates" (Part VII intro).

- "Team objectives are comprised of an objective (the problem that needs to be solved) and some measures of progress (the key results)" (Ch 53, "Empowerment"). The objective is qualitative, "the quantitative dimension will be discussed in the key results" (Ch 53). Examples: "Reduce the frequency of parcels delivered to the wrong address"; "Reduce the time required to deploy a new or updated service to production" (Ch 53). "'We need an app' vs. 'Our users need to be able to access our services from anywhere'" (Ch 50).
- Leaders set the objective, the team sets the numbers: "it is the responsibility of the leaders to decide which problems should be worked on by which product teams" (Ch 54); "if the leaders want the product team to feel ownership of the results, then the key results must come from the team" (Ch 54); "if we were to provide to the team explicit measures of success including timeframe, they won't feel that ownership" (Ch 53). Iterated: "Remember not to get too attached to the specific wording of the objective" (Ch 53).
- Carries: "between two and four key results for each objective. The first is normally the primary measure. Then one or more as a measure of quality, sometimes called guardrail or backstop key results" (Ch 53). Worked: "Reduce percentage of incorrect deliveries / While ensuring that total deliveries continue to grow / While ensuring the cost of delivery does not grow" (Ch 53). Strategic context is mandatory: "it's critical that the team have a deep understanding of the ultimate goal and why this is an important problem to solve" (Ch 53); constraints travel with it, "ensuring the solution does not violate an existing contract or compliance constraint" (Ch 50). Ambition is stated: roof shot, "lower-risk, but highly likely, tangible results," or moon shot, "a 10X improvement"; some attach "80 percent likely to achieve a roof shot versus 20 percent likely to achieve a moon shot" (Ch 55). A high-integrity commitment, where one exists, is "tracked independently of the key results" (Ch 56).
- Verbatim from the case study (Ch 67): "Objective: Improve success rate for employers via premium services. KR: Improve employer success rate (for test cohort) from 37% to 40%. KR: Listings revenue impact neutral or positive (for test cohort)." A leading-indicator form: "Objective: Demonstrate product/market fit for enterprise. KR: Get at least 8 customer discovery program customers to sign letter of intent to buy." A platform team inheriting: "KR (inherit key results of Enterprise Tools team)." Each is preceded by one sentence of theory: "they believed this because they had evidence that these features generate more successful postings."
- Never: features as objectives, "they are problems to solve and not features to build" (Ch 53); activities as key results, "it is very easy to ship a deliverable yet not solve the underlying problem" (Ch 53); "code complete by the end of the quarter" is "output, not outcomes" (Ch 54); what is easy to measure over what is meaningful, "let the tail wag the dog" (Ch 53); dates except on commitments, "pretty soon your objectives become nothing more than a list of deliverables and dates, which is little more than a reformatted roadmap" (Ch 56); manager and individual objectives (Part VII intro).
- Success: "we define success by business results (aka outcome) and not simply activity or output" (Ch 53). Baseline to target on a named measure. When the measure is unknown, "the team may need some time to better understand the dynamics and the most appropriate measures" (Ch 53). Weekly check-ins (Ch 58). Accountability scales with ambition; a missed roof shot is treated "much the same way we treat an outage" (Ch 59). "If we tell the team the feature we want them to build, then if that feature doesn't provide the necessary results, we can't hold the team accountable" (Ch 53). Commitments "are binary" and dated only after discovery, "usually a few days" (Ch 56).
- Assumptions and confidence: objectives are bets, "primarily on the people, but also on new enabling technologies, changing market conditions and customer behaviors, and the strength of the insights" (Ch 55). "The confidence level will, of course, be low in this first quarter" (Ch 54). "You especially don't want anyone to assume that something is a very-high-confidence result when it's not" (Ch 55). The four risks appear at the commitment gate, "valuable, usable, feasible, and viable" (Ch 56).
- Below: "the teams are expected to try out potential solutions in discovery and pursue solutions where they have evidence they will work" (Ch 67). Nothing on how an objective appears in a backlog tool. Keep-the-lights-on has no objective and is expected: "fixing critical problems, responding to customer issues, providing assistance to other teams, technical debt work, and more" (Ch 54). Platform work inherits the consumer's objective (Ch 56).
- Where it breaks: "If the company is still using feature teams, as most unfortunately are, then the OKR technique is going to be a cultural mismatch, and almost certainly prove a waste of time and effort" (Part VII intro). "The role of leadership is largely missing in action. They literally think that the idea is to let teams identify a set of objectives" (Part VII intro). Teams inventing their own objectives is the failure, not the fallback (Ch 54). "Admittedly, these are two big assumptions" (Ch 60). What survives without the technique: "a knowledgeable leader to sit down with the relevant product teams, explain the strategic context, and then tell each team which problems you need them to work on, and what business results they should measure" (Ch 50). Tech leads covering the PM seat: "the business complexities and constraints overwhelmed the tech leads" (Ch 68). He offers nothing to a team with no objective handed to it.
- Against the tool: "companies think they can 'check the empowerment box' by giving the team objectives, yet still continue to tell them the solutions" (Part VII intro). "What is important is (1) their focus on a small number of meaningful objectives, and (2) they are measuring the results based on business results, not output or activities" (Ch 53). "Whether you use a formal technique like OKRs is much less important. The key is to have this conversation" (Ch 60). The written objective "is the result of the negotiation between the product leaders and the product teams" (Ch 67). A tool that fills in key-result values is doing the thing he says removes ownership.

### wayfinder, Matt Pocock

Read 2026-09-18 at github.com/mattpocock/skills, HEAD c55ee46; the skill files last changed 2026-08-19. MIT, copyright 2026 Matt Pocock. Renamed from `decision-mapping` in v1.1. Prior art for the shape of a large-work record, not a product artifact.

- The map is one tracker issue with child issues; "the canonical artifact." Five sections: Destination, "one or two lines; every session orients to it before choosing a ticket"; Notes; Decisions so far; Not yet specified; Out of scope (SKILL.md).
- "The map is an index, not a store. It lists the decisions made and points at the tickets that hold their detail; a decision lives in exactly one place, its ticket, so the map never restates it, only gists it and links." Open tickets "are not listed: they are open child issues, found by query" (SKILL.md).
- Every child is a decision, "questions whose resolution is a decision, not slices of a build to execute." Four types: research, prototype, grilling, task; task "is the one type that does rather than decides, and it earns its place by unblocking a decision" (SKILL.md). Ordered by the tracker's native blocking; the frontier is "the open, unblocked, unclaimed children."

## What an epic carries

The consensus of the four books, ordered by how many agree and how cheap the item is. Each names its sources. Everything at this level is a guess written down, and every book says so.

- A verb-phrase title that distills the why, never a feature name. Patton's "Improve entering and editing comments," Cagan's "Reduce the frequency of parcels delivered to the wrong address." No book disagrees.
- The need, in the words of the people who have it, with more than one way to meet it. Torres' opportunity, Cagan's problem to solve, Patton's opportunity, Gilad's goal. A feature request is converted, never carried as is.
- Who, specifically and plural. All four. Cagan and Hoskins, from story, add who it is not for.
- The outcome as a behavior of those people, with a number and a direction: baseline to target. Cagan and Gilad "from 37% to 40%"; Torres a number, a direction, a behavior; Patton a target outcome in a sentence plus metrics named before release. When it cannot be measured yet, the honest form is a learning goal or "TBD, by when": Torres, Patton, Gilad all allow it.
- One outcome, with a guardrail. Cagan's two to four key results, the first primary and the rest backstops; Torres' health metric; Lean UX's one outcome per statement. Gilad's "fewer is better."
- The why chain, one or two sentences: this addresses the need because; meeting the need moves the outcome because. Torres' walk-the-lines, Cagan's one sentence of theory before each objective, Gilad's context on the goal, Patton's "how we imagine it'll benefit our organization."
- The two or three leap-of-faith assumptions, phrased as what must be true, each with the evidence it rests on in a few words. Torres' upper-right corner, Patton's "biggest, riskiest," Gilad's riskiest first, Cagan's four risks. Never the twenty to thirty.
- The confidence as a word and its strongest evidence, never a number. Gilad's own communicated form, "medium-low, our strongest evidence is support requests"; Cagan's roof shot or moon shot and "don't let anyone assume high confidence"; Patton's "stop pretending."
- The children as an index, with whitespace for pieces that have no story yet. Patton's bullets under a distilled title and activities with no tasks; wayfinder's Not yet specified; the earlier suite's "placeholder, scope TBD." Never pre-split.
- Constraints that travel with the problem, only when real. Cagan's contracts and compliance; Patton's context hung around the map.
- Pointers to evidence and decisions, never the content. Torres' snapshots, Gilad's links to steps and results, wayfinder's index. Cagan's strategic context is the one thing that must be present, not linked.
- A budget in days, weeks, or months, as a budget and not an estimate. Patton only. Cagan's S, M, L from Inspired sits at the same level. Story pushed size to the tracker's field; whether the epic does the same is open.
- What is out of scope, when a boundary was drawn. Wayfinder's section; Patton's "cutting away more than you keep"; Torres' siblings not chosen. Story cut non-goals; whether the epic keeps them is open.

## What an epic never carries

No dissent among the four.

- A feature list or a solution posing as the need. Torres, Patton, Cagan, Gilad.
- The spec, the requirements, the narrative. Patton, Gilad, Cagan.
- Dates or commitments, except one named high-integrity commitment tracked apart from the outcome. Cagan, Gilad, Patton.
- Activities or deliverables as the measure of success. Cagan, Torres, Gilad.
- The children pre-split into sprint-sized pieces, or the whole plan. Patton, Gilad, wayfinder.
- Numbers written by anyone other than the team that owns them. Cagan; Gilad's gaming warnings.
- A score presented as a decision. Torres, Gilad.
- ROI. Patton.
- Two parents. Torres.
- Theme or strategy alignment as its justification. Gilad.

## How success is stated

The books agree on the form and differ on where it lives.

- Form: a change in what specific people do, measured after release, baseline to target, leading over lagging, one at a time with a guardrail, instrumented before release and reviewed after. Test criteria are counts set before the test, "3 out of 10," never percentages.
- Honesty: "unmeasured, here is what we want to learn" is a legitimate line. Patton's learning release, Torres' learning goal before performance goal, Gilad's "KR: TBD" with a date, Cagan's "the team may need some time to understand the most appropriate measures."
- Confidence rides with it. Gilad in a word with the strongest evidence; Cagan as ambition level; Patton as "we're just guessing"; Torres as the leap-of-faith list.
- Where: Patton and Cagan put the outcome on the unit itself. Torres and Gilad put it one level above, shared by sibling opportunities or ideas. A plain Jira shop has no level above the epic, so the epic either carries its outcome or names the one it inherits, and a parentless epic with no outcome line is triage's symptom, as a parentless story is.
- Done for the whole: Patton's grade per activity and "release when the scale balances"; Cagan's key results met or the postmortem; Torres never, "you are never done with discovery"; wayfinder "nothing left to decide"; the earlier suite never says.

## Where they disagree

- **Level.** Torres and Gilad: outcome above, epic below. Patton and Cagan: outcome on the unit. Gilad puts Jira epics a level lower still, at the test step. The resolution for a shop with one level above the story: the epic carries the outcome line, and says whose it is.
- **Who writes the number.** Cagan: the team, or ownership is lost. Torres: a negotiation between leader and trio. Gilad: the PM-owned bank. Every position forbids the tool from filling it. The baseline is a fact the skill can look up; the target is a decision it asks.
- **Assumption kinds.** Torres five, Gilad Cagan's four with no ethical kind, Patton two: users and technical. All agree only the two or three leap-of-faith ones belong on the record, and Torres says the categories do not matter.
- **Confidence.** Gilad an additive number on a 0 to 10 scale; Cagan roof shot or moon shot, sometimes a percentage; Torres refuses to mathematize; Patton says everything at this level is a guess. Gilad himself communicates it as a word plus the strongest evidence.
- **What is below.** Torres and Patton: stories from a story map of use. Gilad: steps, mostly tests, before stories. Wayfinder: decisions, never build slices. The earlier suite: an owner-tagged breakdown. Cagan says nothing.
- **Success on the record at all.** The four books: yes, or the unit is a roadmap item. Wayfinder and the earlier suite: no outcome line; done is "nothing left to decide" or "what, why, pieces, owners."
- **Out of scope.** Wayfinder keeps a section. Torres keeps the unchosen siblings on the tree. Story cut non-goals as explaining rather than deciding.
- **Owners on the pieces.** The earlier suite tags each piece with a delivery-side owner. No book does. Story decided the skills never model who is running them.

## What cuts against the tool itself

- Everything an epic says is a guess, and a fluent record overstates it. Gilad: nothing written by one person can raise confidence past 0.1; Torres: a coherent story tells you only that someone constructed one; Patton: "stop pretending." An epic the skill drafts has to carry its evidence level in words or it is salesmanship in a template.
- The numbers are the team's. Cagan's whole argument for the form is ownership of the key results. The skill looks up the baseline and asks for the target, with what it costs if wrong and who could help; it never proposes the number.
- The need has to have been heard, not invented. Torres: "are we just wishing a customer would say this?" In a plain Jira shop the epic arrives as a handed-down feature, and every book's fallback is the same two questions asked backward: what would this do for them, and what would it do for the business. Patton adds the move: write the guesses down, share them with the decision makers, "they'll correct you."
- No-go is a result. Patton's opportunity discussion exists "to decide whether to move forward with them, or trash them"; Torres' fruit of discovery is the time saved; Gilad parks most ideas. The skill should be able to end at "this epic should not exist," and say who decides that.
- Placeholder is legitimate. Patton's whitespace, wayfinder's fog, the earlier suite's "scope TBD." An epic with a title, a need, and no outcome yet is not a deficiency; an epic that has been that for a quarter is triage's finding.
- The level above is missing. Cagan and Torres both assume a leader who hands down the problem and the strategic context. When nobody does, Cagan offers nothing and Torres offers the backward questions. The skill fills the gap with facts it can find, not with invented strategy.
- The record is an index. Wayfinder's rule, Torres' snapshots, Gilad's links: detail lives in exactly one place and the epic points at it. That is the answer to the PRD question: the epic is what a stakeholder reads in a minute, Torres' one paragraph, and everything else is a link or a child.
- Keep-the-lights-on has no epic. Cagan expects it, Gilad keeps technical ideas out of the bank, Patton does not sweat the small stuff. Consistent with story's parentless stories.
