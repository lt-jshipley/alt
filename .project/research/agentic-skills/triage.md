### Thinking in Systems

The source for the principle. Her unit is the system, her intervention is structural, and her intervenor has time and power ours does not.

- The twelve leverage points, least to most effective (Leverage Points): numbers, buffers, stock-and-flow structures, delays, balancing loops, reinforcing loops, information flows, rules, self-organization, goals, paradigms, transcending paradigms. "Numbers, the sizes of flows, are dead last on my list of powerful interventions. Diddling with the details, arranging the deck chairs on the Titanic. Probably 90—no 95, no 99 percent—of our attention goes to parameters, but there's not a lot of leverage in them." And the caveat: "It's not that parameters aren't important—they can be, especially in the short term and to the individual who's standing directly in the flow." "Parameters become leverage points when they go into ranges that kick off one of the items higher on this list."
- Information flows: "Missing information flows is one of the most common causes of system malfunction." "Adding or restoring information can be a powerful intervention, usually much easier and cheaper than rebuilding physical infrastructure." "It's important that the missing feedback be restored to the right place and in compelling form ... it's not enough to inform all the users of an aquifer that the groundwater level is dropping. That could initiate a race to the bottom." Why loops go missing: "There is a systematic tendency on the part of human beings to avoid accountability for their own decisions. That's why there are so many missing feedback loops—and why this kind of leverage point is so often popular with the masses, unpopular with the powers that be, and effective, if you can get the powers that be to permit it to happen (or go around them and make it happen anyway)."
- Rules and goals: "If you want to understand the deepest malfunctions of systems, pay attention to the rules and to who has power over them." "Purposes are deduced from behavior, not from rhetoric or stated goals" (The Basics). "Before you disturb the system in any way, watch how it behaves" (Living in a World of Systems).
- A count is a stock (The Basics): "A stock is the memory of the history of changing flows within the system." "if you see a behavior that persists over time, there is likely a mechanism creating that consistent behavior." "A stock can be increased by decreasing its outflow rate as well as by increasing its inflow rate." Events versus structure (Why Systems Surprise Us): "long-term behavior provides clues to the underlying system structure."
- The traps (System Traps ... and Opportunities). "Blaming, disciplining, firing, twisting policy levers harder, hoping for a more favorable sequence of driving events, tinkering at the margins—these standard responses will not fix structural problems." Drift to low performance: "When perceived performance slips, the goal is allowed to slip. 'Well, that's about all you can expect.'" Way out: "Keep performance standards absolute. Even better, let standards be enhanced by the best actual performances instead of being discouraged by the worst." Rule beating: "perverse behavior that gives the appearance of obeying the rules or achieving the goals, but that actually distorts the system," "usually a response of the lower levels in a hierarchy to overrigid, deleterious, unworkable, or ill-defined rules from above"; the way out is to "understand rule beating as useful feedback, and to revise, improve, rescind, or better explain the rules." Seeking the wrong goal: "Systems ... have a terrible tendency to produce exactly and only what you ask them to produce"; "Be especially careful not to confuse effort with result." Shifting the burden to the intervenor: "A well-meaning and efficient intervenor watches the struggle and steps in to take some of the load ... Congratulations are in order, usually self-congratulations by the intervenor to the intervenor. Then the original problem reappears." Way out: "If you are the intervenor, work in such a way as to restore or enhance the system's own ability to solve its problems, then remove yourself."
- Information that triggers action: "information about the existence of a problem may be necessary but not sufficient to trigger action—information about resources, incentives, and consequences is necessary too" (The Basics). "'Intrinsic responsibility' means that the system is designed to send feedback about the consequences of decision making directly and quickly and compellingly to the decision makers" (Living).
- The limiting factor (Why Systems Surprise Us): "At any given time, the input that is most important to a system is the one that is most limiting." "It does no good to pour on more phosphorus, if the problem is low potassium."
- Against the report itself (Living): "if we motivate ourselves, rate ourselves, and reward ourselves on our ability to produce quantity, then quantity will be the result." "Pretending that something doesn't exist if it's hard to quantify leads to faulty models." "Before you charge in to make things better, pay attention to the value of what's already there." "starting with history discourages the common and distracting tendency we all have to define a problem not by the system's actual behavior, but by the lack of our favorite solution." "Systems thinking can only tell us to do that. It can't do it."
- The low-power actor: the car dealer, "even without the ability to change that part of her system, the dealer can learn to manage inventory quite well" (Systems Zoo). "Blaming the individual rarely helps." The two interventions she shows working without authority are both information put in front of the decider: the front-hall electricity meter, and the newspaper "reporters, who had a tendency to make lists of 'the top ten local polluters.' That's all that happened," after which "chemical emissions nationwide ... had decreased by 40 percent" (Leverage Points).

### Inspired, first edition

Ranking sits above the product manager, decisions are go or no-go, and the book excludes our shop by name. "Riskiest assumption" does not occur in this edition; "risk" appears thirteen times, none as an ordering rule.

- Who ranks: "it's the responsibility of the product team to assess the benefits and the costs. Then it's the responsibility of the management team (see the chapter The Product Council) to ensure the company is pursuing the best opportunities available" (Ch 11, Assessing Product Opportunities). The council issues go or no-go at four milestones and "There is no need to review minor updates or fixes in this process—there should be an expedited process for the minor changes needed to run the business" (Ch 14, The Product Council).
- The only ordering rule is about goals, not items: "get the team to agree on the specific ordering, most to least important. Don't just wave your hands and group the goals into something like 'critical' and 'very important.'" Fights are "because each person has a different interpretation or weighting of the goals and the priorities" (Ch 13, Product Principles). "yank all those P1/P2/P3 annotations from the spec" (Ch 20, Minimal Product).
- Saying no: "It is just as important that bad product ideas get identified and rejected at this stage" (Ch 11). Shelving after a test "saving the company the wasted cost of building and shipping a losing product, not to mention the opportunity cost" (Ch 22, Prototype Testing). And the opposite error: "when they run into difficulties with the particular solution they are pursuing, they abandon the opportunity. It's a classic example of throwing the baby out with the bathwater" (Ch 11).
- Specials: "One of the surest ways to derail a product company is to confuse customer requirements with product requirements." "By pursuing these special features now, what important work are you delaying? What is the business cost of that delay?" "It doesn't take too many specials to weigh down a company to the point where it takes them months to do even the smallest release" (Ch 32, Beware of Specials). Requirements tools "make it extremely easy to fall into the trap ... In the worst case, they institutionalize the misguided practices."
- What an unhealthy process is a symptom of: "Most product organizations are essentially feature factories (with some bug fixing thrown in)" (Ch 23, Improving Existing Products). "By far the most common reason product managers request changes to the spec is a consequence of not really thinking through the requirements in the first place" (Ch 20). "it is all too easy for the mere existence of the spec to serve as a false indicator to management and the product team that everything is proceeding just fine" (Ch 18). "Everyone assumes the objectives and the priority" (Ch 13). "the root cause of wasted releases can most often be traced to poor definition of the role of the product manager" (Ch 2).
- Open questions during build are expected, and the fix is speed: "jump on their questions and get answers as fast as humanly possible" (Ch 5). "the remote developers are forced to make assumptions about who wanted what ... there should be no question to whom the engineering team is accountable" (Ch 5).
- Managing up (Ch 10): "Recommendations, not issues." "Use data and facts, not opinions ... 'If we're going to make this decision based on opinions, we're going to use my opinion.'" "Measure and plan for churn ... track how much of your week/month/quarter is spent on forward progress." And "at least you know who you really need to convince ... does she base her decisions on a demo, or market data, or on customer commitments" (Ch 30). Decisions handed down: "doing a lightweight and quick product opportunity assessment is still valuable ... at least you will have a clear understanding of your objective" (Ch 11).
- Metrics decide: "the justification for the new feature is the degree to which it can improve these metrics." "What matters is what actually moves the needle on the metrics you are driving towards" (Ch 23). "you need to know what results you would consider to be a success and what results you would consider to be a problem" (Ch 25, Rapid Response).
- Out of scope, explicit: not for "custom software projects" (Introduction); "In the custom software model, since the customer believes he knows what he needs, you'll rarely find the role of the product manager" (Ch 26). His remedy for a PM who cannot decide is escalate or replace.

### Accelerate

The only voice against ceremony. Read for what keeps triage from becoming a gate.

- Small batches: "Even though working in small chunks adds some overhead, it reaps enormous rewards by allowing us to avoid work that delivers zero or negative value" (Ch 4, Technical Practices). "in software, batch size is hard to measure and communicate across contexts as there is no visible inventory" (Ch 2, Measuring Performance).
- WIP limits and visibility (Ch 7, Management Practices for Software): "WIP limits on their own do not strongly predict delivery performance. It's only when they're combined with the use of visual displays and have a feedback loop from production monitoring tools back to delivery teams or the business that we see a strong effect." "WIP limits are no good if they don't lead to improvements that increase flow." What displays carry: "the types of information being displayed, how broadly it is being shared, and how easy it is to access."
- Change approval (Ch 7): "approval by an external body (such as a manager or CAB) simply doesn't work to increase the stability of production systems ... However, it certainly slows things down. It is, in fact, worse than having no change approval process at all." "This idea is a form of risk management theater: we check boxes so that when something goes wrong, we can say that at least we followed the process." Where outsiders belong: "this is more of a governance role than actually inspecting changes. Such teams should be monitoring delivery performance and helping teams improve it."
- Authority over the spec (Ch 8, Product Development): "if a development team isn't allowed, without authorization from some outside body, to change requirements or specifications in response to what they discover, their ability to innovate is sharply inhibited." Handed-down work as the anti-pattern (Ch 10): "requirements are handed down to development teams who must then deliver large stacks of work in batches. In this model, employees feel little control over the products they build."
- Culture (Ch 3, Measuring and Changing Culture): good information "provides answers to the questions that the receiver needs answered. It is timely. It is presented in such a way that it can be effectively used by the receiver." "accident investigations that stop at 'human error' are not just bad but dangerous. Human error should, instead, be the start of the investigation." "in pathological and bureaucratic organizational cultures, measurement is used as a form of control, and people hide information that challenges existing rules ... 'whenever there is fear, you get the wrong numbers'" (Ch 2).
- Measurement (Ch 2): bad metrics "focus on outputs rather than outcomes" and "on individual or local measures rather than team or global ones"; velocity, "teams inevitably work to game their velocity"; utilization, "as utilization approaches 100%, lead times approach infinity." "Most maturity models simply measure the technical proficiency or tooling install base in an organization without tying it to outcomes. These end up being vanity metrics" (Ch 1). "We suspect this is due to attempts to increase tempo ('work harder!') which fail to address the underlying obstacles" (Ch 2).
- Flow end to end: "Whether teams have a good understanding of the flow of work from the business all the way through to customers, and whether they have visibility into this flow, including the status of products and features" (Ch 8). The "fuzzy front end" of lead time, "often unclear when to start the clock" (Ch 2). "failure demand— demand for work caused by the failure to do the right thing the first time" (Ch 4). "Knowledge is power, and you should give power to those who have the knowledge" (Ch 11).

### Continuous Discovery Habits, the choosing chapters

Compare a set, never score, time-box, and treat the choice as reversible. The shape of the record is a diagnostic.

- Against whether-or-not (Ch 2): "Product trios get caught up in 'whether or not' decisions when we react to one customer need or pain point at a time ... We also encounter 'whether or not' decisions when a stakeholder asks us to implement their pet feature ... This is perhaps the most common mistake product trios make." "Instead of asking, 'Should we solve this customer need?' we'll ask, 'Which of these customer needs is most important for us to address right now?'"
- The comparison (Ch 7, Prioritizing Opportunities, Not Solutions): "compare and contrast the set of parent opportunities against each other." Four lenses, "opportunity sizing, market factors, company factors, and customer factors"; sizing is "How many customers are affected and how often? ... we want to size a set of siblings against each other"; company factors include "the company's political climate. We might need to spend a lot of political capital." "You might be tempted to score each opportunity ... Don't do this. This is a messy, subjective decision, and you want to keep it that way." "Think about each set of criteria as a different lens through which to view impact. Use the lenses to fuel your conversation." Anti-patterns: "Over-relying on one set of factors," "Working backwards from your expected conclusion," and "Delaying a decision until there is more data ... time-box your decision. Give yourself an hour or two or, at most, a day or two."
- Reversible (Ch 7, Ch 11): "You are not committing to addressing that opportunity ... you'll simply turn around and choose another target opportunity." "when people viewed a decision as reversible, they continued to critically evaluate their decision after making it." "'We killed an opportunity on Tuesday, chose a new one on Wednesday.'"
- The shape diagnoses (Ch 2): "If our opportunity space is too shallow, it can guide us to do more customer interviews. A sprawling opportunity space, on the other hand, reminds us to narrow our focus. If we aren't considering enough solutions for our target opportunity, we can hold an ideation session." A flat list: "it can be hard to prioritize a flat list of opportunities, because opportunities come in different shapes and sizes. Some opportunities are interrelated, while others are subsets of others" (Ch 6). "This obsession with producing outputs is strangling us. It's why we spend countless hours prioritizing features, grooming backlogs, and micro-managing releases" (Ch 7). "If we spread ourselves too thin across many opportunities, we'll find ourselves right back in the waterfall mindset."
- Stakeholders (Ch 13, Show Your Work): "we are inviting our stakeholders to an opinion battle—a battle we have no chance of winning ... Many product trios complain about the HiPPO but miss the role they play in creating this situation." Routing a request: "is the stakeholder focused on a different outcome from you? If yes, then don't shoot down their idea ... 'That idea has promise. We'll consider it when we address that opportunity.' You can even capture it on your tree or in your idea backlog (not your development backlog)." "If you can see that it is based on a faulty assumption, don't just call that out. Help your stakeholder reach that conclusion on their own." "Instead of presenting a conclusion, we are generating and evaluating options." Without authority: "Choose your battles. Don't fight the ones you can't win"; "you have more agency than you think you do" (Ch 14).

### Evidence Guided, the choosing chapters

He grades, then tells you not to trust the grade. The idea bank's states are the nearest thing to a tracker-health specification on the shelf, and the order belongs to the team.

- The bank (Ch 3, Ideas): "Idea banks are not backlogs, meaning we don't expect to implement all or even most of the ideas they hold. Their main functions are to keep track of ideas, to help us compare them quickly, and to remind us that there's always more than one option." "Anyone can suggest an idea, but only the idea bank owner (typically the product manager) can enter it ... treating the idea bank as a suggestion box will make it unusable very quickly." "Technical ideas that don't directly impact any of the team's metrics ... should be collected and prioritized separately by the engineering lead."
- States and cadence (Ch 3): "After an initial evaluation, or triage, you may choose to park some ideas ... (it's good practice to explain your reasoning in the idea record). Parking is the right choice for the majority of incoming ideas. You should keep a much smaller list (typically no more than 40) of Candidates ... From time to time, say once a quarter, you should go over the Candidates and Parked lists and decide whether to demote or promote." "spending just a few minutes evaluating each new idea during the initial triage ... You definitely don't need to bring the entire development team or any managers or stakeholders." "the idea bank is not just our repository of ideas, but also our knowledge base of learning" (Ch 4).
- The score (Ch 3, Ch 4): "just sorting ideas by ICE scores can lead to naive decisions ... consider ICE scores as mere hints ... if you see an idea you believe in, feel free to pick it out of order." "the three components of ICE ... were far more helpful than the total ICE score, which created a number of false signals ... You don't necessarily have to answer these questions with numbers, but you do have to be rigorous and consistent." "that's exactly the goal of ICE—to facilitate a structured discussion." "Many business teams complain about product managers making 'black-box decisions' that lack clear reasoning."
- Stopping (Ch 4, Ch 5): "Most of the time you'll find that your idea created no measurable improvement, or that the results are not clear. In both cases you should assume that the idea is not working. Try to involve more people in this discussion, especially ones that have no skin in the game." "once we've committed, invested, and launched, our ability to abandon the idea or make major corrections is very limited. Mostly we're motivated to call it a success and move on." In flight: "Some ideas may be already in-flight from the previous quarter, but you should ask if these still represent the best investment." "There should be no hidden projects." Every task linked upward: "keep managing tasks in whatever tool you're using today—Jira ... but start referencing or linking back to the steps."
- Who orders (Ch 5): "it's best to leave the decision about which ideas to pursue and in which order to the team ... The team is choosing which ideas to test first, not which ideas to build and launch ... The team is allowed to say 'no' to ideas that fall outside the goals." "experience, expertise, and rank don't improve the odds of success" (Ch 3).
- Senior requests (Ch 3, Ch 6): "In some organizations you may have to pick an idea just because a senior or influential person requests it. This shouldn't be a problem as long as these are rare occurrences ... picking an idea does not mean committing to building and launching it, but rather putting it into validation and acting on the evidence. No idea should be exempt from this rule." "Now people routinely ask, 'What's the evidence?' even for ideas coming from the CEO."
- Doing it wrong (Ch 9, Adopting GIST): "If you find that you're spending hours each week on ICE-scoring ideas, or taking six steps to validate a one-week feature, you're probably doing it wrong." "beware of innovative ways to 'improve' the process that are actually wrangling power back ('The opinion of the CMO should be considered market data')." "weed out any goals—explicit or implicit—that are about launch dates, velocity, scope, utilization, story points, or any other form of output." Metrics that should go down: "Time spent on planning / Number of sales escalation or management mandates per quarter." Without authority: "start measuring the key metrics right now ... The current numbers may be depressingly low, but this is normal. Use this fact as evidence."

## What a count is a symptom of

The question the outline left open, answered by Meadows and confirmed from the other four. A count is a stock; a stock that persists has a mechanism behind it; the mechanism is never the count. Our own symptoms, the ones story and epic leave on the record on purpose, sort into her traps.

- **Parentless stories, a story whose Open routes to "Parent: none," a title-only epic that has been one for a quarter.** Rule beating: the lower level working around a rule it cannot follow, because nobody above has stated a need for the work to hang under. Her way out is to read it "as useful feedback" and fix the rule or the gap above, never to enforce harder. Cagan names the gap: "Everyone assumes the objectives and the priority."
- **Stale items, spec drifted into comments, an Open line nobody answered.** Drift to low performance: "that's about all you can expect." Cagan's fix is speed of answer, not a queue. Meadows' standard is absolute, "enhanced by the best actual performances instead of being discouraged by the worst."
- **An epic unmeasured with work under it, a child that does not serve its epic's need, a backlog of stakeholder requests.** Seeking the wrong goal: effort confused with result; Torres' whether-or-not decisions taken one request at a time; Cagan's feature factory; Gilad's output goals. The wound is a goal nobody holds, and every source's fallback is the same: name the outcome the work is supposed to move and who holds that number.
- **A team that is busy and nothing moves.** Policy resistance and utilization: Accelerate's "as utilization approaches 100%, lead times approach infinity," and "attempts to increase tempo ('work harder!') which fail to address the underlying obstacles."
- **Underneath all of them, one wound.** A decision, a need, or a measure that nobody in the room holds. That is what a count is a symptom of in a shop with no product owner, and it is why the routed line carries a holder.

## What triage decides, and what it never decides

- **It never grades.** Story and epic refuse a verdict on the item; a graded ledger is Meadows' parameter and Accelerate's vanity metric, and the earlier suite's own line applies: an indictment gets defended in a meeting. The count is honest and secondary.
- **It never orders.** Torres: "you want to keep it that way." Gilad: order belongs to the team, scores are hints. Cagan: ranking sits above the PM. A tool that orders is the one that "stops thinking," and our runner does not hold the decision anyway.
- **It fixes what the record settles.** A parent the thread names, a baseline the measures seat holds, an Open line the thread answered: facts, not questions, and one edit each in the sibling's shape. Meadows: parameters matter "to the individual who's standing directly in the flow." Gilad: six steps for a one-week feature is doing it wrong.
- **It routes what nobody holds.** When many symptoms share one cause that is a decision, one Open line on the epic that owns it, holder and if-wrong, with the symptoms as evidence under it. When no epic exists, one message to the holder. Meadows' two working interventions were information put in front of the decider; Cagan's "recommendations, not issues."
- **It says what is working.** Meadows: "pay attention to the value of what's already there." The earlier suite: never padding. It is the counterweight that keeps fair wording from reading rosier than the record.
- **It leaves fine alone.** An item with nothing wrong gets nothing written. Zero findings is a success.

## Relationship to story, epic, examine, and orient

- **Story and epic leave the symptoms; triage reads them.** The count of parentless stories, the "Parent: none" route, the unmeasured epic with children, the child that does not serve the need, the placeholder that stayed one. Triage is weakest on a backlog nobody has refined, and its first finding there is "nothing carries a need or a holder," which is still the wound.
- **The routed line is epic's Open line.** Holder's hat and name, the decision, if-wrong. The earlier suite's routed-question contract, question as a decision, owner rung plus name, exposure while open, where the answer lives, is the same thing from the other side; our Open line already carries three of its four fields and the record is the fourth.
- **The record is the state.** No ledger file. On re-run, the Open lines triage wrote are either still there unanswered or they are gone, and movement is read the way story-refine reads an answered Open line out of the thread.
- **Examine is not invoked.** Triage writes the small edits itself in the siblings' shape. An item that needs a full rework gets one line naming story-refine or epic-refine, the way orient recommends and never invokes.
- **Orient's seats are triage's reach.** Tracker for the items and their threads, record for what was decided, measures for the baseline, docs for what the items link, people never asked. A seat the file lists but the session cannot reach is a look-this-up line, as orient does.

## What cuts against the tool itself

- **Every book says the tool must not decide.** Torres' lenses "fuel your conversation"; Gilad's ICE exists "to facilitate a structured discussion"; Meadows' systems thinking "can only tell us to do that. It can't do it." A triage that hands down a list has become the external body Accelerate measured.
- **The intervenor trap is this skill's own failure mode.** A tool that finds a wound under every count, and sends a team off for a quarter when a one-line answer was right, is Meadows' intervenor congratulating itself. The fixture runs of story and epic already showed examine leaning that way. Fix the small thing.
- **Information alone does not trigger action.** Meadows: "information about resources, incentives, and consequences is necessary too." The if-wrong line is the consequence; the holder is the incentive; the report is not finished until each finding has both.
- **The compelling form is the one the holder already reads.** A business user does not open a markdown file, and a PDF is a chat thread with a border. A comment on the item renders, notifies, and sits on the thing it is about. HTML is the one-line offer for a scope-level share; it is the leave-behind, not the state.
- **Read-only was chosen for a reason that no longer holds.** The earlier suite wrote nothing because the write would have been indiscriminate across a client's board. A write of two or three lines to the items that own the wound, shown first, one yes, is a different thing.
- **The books exclude our shop.** Cagan by name; Gilad's GIST "may not work in a very traditional organization"; Torres' own concession is Chapter 14. What survives is what a low-power actor can do: watch first, do not blame, put the fact in front of the decider, choose your battles.

## Decided 2026-09-21

In conversation after the reading, before the examine run.

- The value: triage tells the person who holds a decision what their backlog is a symptom of, and what it costs them each week it stays that way. Not which tickets are unworkable; story-refine and examine's readiness read already do that per item.
- Triage neither grades nor orders. It reads a scope, names the mechanism behind each count, fixes what the record settles, and routes what nobody holds.
- Fixing and routing are not either-or. The three stories whose threads name a parent get it set, and the eleven with no epic to point at are one wound line. The wound line never excuses leaving the cheap fixes undone.
